\chapter{What Structure Buys, and Where It Stops Paying}
\label{ch:em-results}

Chapter~\ref{ch:em-method} built an estimator that is exact in its inference
step and approximate only in its numerical representation and its optimisation
budget. Chapter~\ref{ch:em-kernel} measured what it costs to run. This chapter
asks what it is \emph{worth}, and the question is comparative: worth relative to
a network trained on the same clean trajectories and given none of the
structure. What each arm is allowed to observe of those trajectories differs,
and Section~\ref{sec:em-estimand} states how before any number is quoted.

\begin{quote}
How much does supplying the chain reduce denoising error at a fixed number of
clean trajectories; how much of that reduction survives against a baseline that is given
locality but not the model; how much mixture capacity does the estimator need;
and how far can the Markov assumption itself be wrong before the structure
stops paying?
\end{quote}

\noindent The four are answered, at different strengths, and
Section~\ref{sec:em-summary} states each at the strength its evidence supports.

\section{What is compared, and how it is scored}\label{sec:em-estimand}

\subsection{The estimand}

Every headline number in this chapter is a ratio of two relative $L^2$ score
errors.

Fix a diffusion time $t$ and a test set $\{x^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{J}$ of noised
sequences drawn from the model of Section~\ref{sec:em-model}. Let
$\score^{\star}(x,t) = \nabla_x \log p_t(x)$ be the exact joint score, supplied
by the validated grid reference of Section~\ref{sec:l-grid} under the true
kernel. For an estimator $\widehat{\score}$ write
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}\big[\widehat{\score}; t\big]
  \;=\;
  \Bigg(
  \frac{\sum_{j=1}^{J}\norm{\widehat{\score}(x^{(j)},t) - \score^{\star}(x^{(j)},t)}^2}
       {\sum_{j=1}^{J}\norm{\score^{\star}(x^{(j)},t)}^2}
  \Bigg)^{1/2},
  \label{eq:em-relerr}
\end{equation}
the ratio of two Euclidean norms over the whole test set, so that $0.1$ means
the estimate is wrong by a tenth of the signal's own size. It is scale free:
multiplying the data by a constant leaves it unchanged. Pooling over the noise
schedule $\mathcal{T}$ means pooling the two sums, not averaging the ratios,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{E}^2\big[\widehat{\score}\big]
  \;=\;
  \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} w_t \,\mathcal{E}^2\big[\widehat{\score}; t\big],
  \qquad
  w_t \;=\;
  \frac{\E\norm{\score^{\star}(X_t,t)}^2}{\sum_{s \in \mathcal{T}}\E\norm{\score^{\star}(X_s,s)}^2}.
  \label{eq:em-pooling}
\end{equation}
The weights are not a free choice and they are not uniform. In the
implementation they are the measured reference score energies of the frozen
test bundle, level by level; the variance-matched Gaussian chain explains
their shape in closed form. There, $\score^{\star} = -\Sigt^{-1}x$ with
$\Sigt = e^{-2t}\Sigz + \Deltat I$, so
\begin{equation}
  \E\norm{\score^{\star}(X_t,t)}^2 \;=\; \operatorname{tr}\Sigt^{-1},
  \qquad
  \Sigt^{-1} \to I \ \ (t \to \infty),
  \label{eq:em-weights}
\end{equation}
and at the low-noise end the limit is \emph{finite}: since
$\Sigz \succ 0$, $\Sigt \to \Sigz$ and
\begin{equation}
  \operatorname{tr}\Sigt^{-1}
  \;=\; \operatorname{tr}\Qz
  \;+\; 2t\,\big[\operatorname{tr}\Qz - \operatorname{tr}\Qz^2\big]
  \;+\; O(t^2)
  \qquad (t \downarrow 0),
  \label{eq:em-weights-smallt}
\end{equation}
with no $1/t$ singularity --- the quantity that does diverge like $1/(2t)$ is
the likelihood precision $e^{-2t}/\Deltat$ in latent coordinates, which is a
different object. The trace nevertheless falls steeply across the schedule
(from $\operatorname{tr}\Qz = 193.4$ at $t \to 0$ to $\nsites = 32$ at
$t \to \infty$ for this $\alpha$ and $\nsites$; the linear coefficient in
\eqref{eq:em-weights-smallt} is $-3140$), so the finite schedule still
weights its lower-noise levels most heavily.
Table~\ref{tab:em-locality} makes that concrete: under the closed-form
weights the four smallest of the twelve levels carry $53.6\%$ of the pooled
weight, and under the measured Laplace energies actually used for pooling the
same four carry $54.5\%$. This matters twice
below --- once when the window head's optimal radius is predicted, and once when
its residual error is discussed.

The two arms are the EM--BP estimator of Chapter~\ref{ch:em-method}, which is
given the Markov factorisation, and a denoising-score-matching network trained
by \eqref{eq:sm-dsm} with no structural information at all. Both are fitted
from the same $\nseq$ clean trajectories, and that --- not ``the same data''
--- is the equality the protocol enforces. What each arm is allowed to see of
those trajectories is not the same, and the difference runs \emph{against} the
result reported here, so it is stated plainly rather than left to the
appendix:

\begin{itemize}
  \item EM--BP sees one noised realisation $x$ of each trajectory and never
  sees the clean trajectory $a$ at all; the noised bundle is frozen before
  fitting begins.
  \item The network is trained on \emph{paired} $(a, x)$ data, as
  \eqref{eq:sm-dsm} requires, and redraws the diffusion time and the
  corrupting noise at every gradient step, so over a run it consumes many
  distinct noisy views of each clean trajectory rather than one.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Supplying the Markov factorisation is thus traded against supplying the clean
signal and unlimited corruptions of it, and both asymmetries are deliberate:
the baseline is the standard training recipe for this model class, and
weakening it to match EM--BP's view would measure a handicap rather than a
structural advantage. The consequence for reading
\eqref{eq:em-ratio} is that the ratio is a statement about
\emph{value per clean trajectory} under the two standard recipes, not about
generic data efficiency at a matched information interface.
Table~\ref{tab:em-architectures} records what each arm in the wider ladder is
given.
Writing $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{base}}$ and $\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{EM}}$ for
their errors on a common test set, the reported quantity is
\begin{equation}
  R \;=\; \frac{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{base}}}{\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{EM}}},
  \qquad R > 1 \ \text{means the structured estimator is more accurate,}
  \label{eq:em-ratio}
\end{equation}
evaluated cell by cell, a cell being one (training-set size, seed) pair, with
both arms re-fitted independently in every cell.

One aggregation choice remains, and it matters because \eqref{eq:em-ratio} is a
ratio of the quantities \eqref{eq:em-relerr} and not a linear functional of
them. This chapter reports exactly the pooled definition above, paired at the
seed level: for each seed and size, squared error and reference energy are
summed over the twelve levels before the square root, giving one pooled error
per arm; the ratio \eqref{eq:em-ratio} is formed within the seed from those
two pooled errors; and the reported number is the mean of the sixteen paired
per-seed ratios, with its standard error. The seed is the inferential unit
throughout --- the twelve levels share a training set and a fitted model.
Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation} recomputes the same cells under four
alternative summaries; the direction and the order of magnitude are the same
in all of them, but the values are not interchangeable: the mean of
per-level ratios, which earlier drafts reported, is systematically the
largest ($\ratiocellwiselo$--$\ratiocellwisehi\times$ on these cells,
against the pooled $\ratiolo$--$\ratiohi\times$), because it lets levels
with little reference energy count as much as levels with a great deal.

\section{The estimators being compared}\label{sec:em-estimators}

\subsection{From a denoiser to a score}

Both network arms are trained by denoising score matching and both are read as
scores, so the choice of output head is a
parameterisation of the same object. If the network predicts the noise,
$\widehat{\varepsilon}_\phi$, or the clean signal, $\widehat{a}_\phi$, then
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{\score}_\varepsilon(x,t) = -\frac{\widehat{\varepsilon}_\phi(x,t)}{\sqrt{\Deltat}},
  \qquad
  \widehat{\score}_{a}(x,t) = \frac{e^{-t}\,\widehat{a}_\phi(x,t) - x}{\Deltat},
  \label{eq:em-parameterisation}
\end{equation}
and the two agree identically under the change of variable
$\widehat{a} = e^{t}(x - \sqrt{\Deltat}\,\widehat{\varepsilon})$. As
\emph{function classes} they are therefore the same; as \emph{optimisation
problems} they are not, because the substitution rescales the residual by
$e^{t}\sqrt{\Deltat}$ and so reweights the noise levels against one another.
Both are offered to every network arm, and the choice is made on validation.
The screen of Section~\ref{sec:em-screen} settles the question empirically and
without ambiguity: within each architecture the best noise-predicting
configuration beats every signal-predicting one under the pooled selection
risk ($0.148$ against $0.224$ for the window head, $0.226$ against $0.427$
for the dilated stack, $0.187$ against $0.262$ for the bidirectional
recurrence). Where a parameterisation is quoted below, it is the noise head.

Throughout, $\tau(t) = \big(t,\ e^{-t},\ \sqrt{\Deltat}\big)$ is the
three-dimensional time embedding, broadcast to every site so that a local
kernel can see it inside its own receptive field; a time feature supplied only
at the sequence ends would be invisible to a convolution.

\subsection{The structured estimator}

EM--BP fits the mixture kernel of \eqref{eq:em-mixture-kernel} by the procedure
of Section~\ref{sec:em-algorithm} and evaluates the score through the
score--posterior identity. Its free parameters are the $C-1$ free mixture
weights, $C$ component means, $C$ component variances and the autoregressive
coefficient, so at $C = 8$
\begin{equation}
  \dim\theta \;=\; (C-1) + C + C + 1 \;=\; 3C \;=\; 24,
  \label{eq:em-dim}
\end{equation}
independent of $\nsites$. Three structural facts are supplied and not learned:
the Markov factorisation, the linear-plus-innovation form of the kernel, and
homogeneity across sites. Everything else --- the innovation law itself, and
$\corr$ --- is estimated from the same noised data the networks see.

\subsection{The unstructured denoiser}

The baseline with no structural prior is a fully connected $\tanh$ network
mapping the whole sequence and the time embedding to the whole output,
\begin{equation}
  f_\phi(x,t) \;=\; W_3\,\tanh\!\big(W_2\,\tanh(W_1 [x;\tau(t)] + b_1) + b_2\big) + b_3,
  \label{eq:em-mlp}
\end{equation}
with $W_1 \in \R^{128\times(\nsites+3)}$, $W_2 \in \R^{128\times128}$,
$W_3 \in \R^{\nsites\times128}$, giving $25{,}248$ free parameters, a thousand
times \eqref{eq:em-dim}. Nothing in \eqref{eq:em-mlp} knows that the sites are
ordered: permuting the coordinates of $x$ and the rows of the output is absorbed
by permuting the columns of $W_1$ and the rows of $W_3$. The network must
rediscover the chain from data, and the comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:em-pointwise} measures what that costs.

\subsection{The three structure-aware architectures}\label{sec:em-archs}

Beating \eqref{eq:em-mlp} says little, because a large part of the margin could
be permutation-blindness rather than the Markov model. Three architectures that
do encode sequence structure are therefore screened, spanning the receptive
field from bounded to unbounded.

\paragraph{Weight-shared window head, radius $r$.} One predictor applied at
every site to a window of its neighbours,
\begin{equation}
  \widehat{y}_k \;=\; g_\phi\big(x_{k-r},\dots,x_{k+r};\ \tau(t)\big),
  \qquad k = 1,\dots,\nsites,
  \label{eq:em-window}
\end{equation}
with $g_\phi$ a two-layer $\tanh$ network of width $W$, the same $\phi$ at every
$k$, and zero padding outside the sequence. This is exactly a one-dimensional
convolution of kernel width $2r+1$ followed by pointwise layers, so it supplies
locality \emph{and} translation invariance and its parameter count,
$(2r+4)W + W^2 + 3W + 1$ --- input layer $(2r+4)W + W$, hidden layer
$W^2 + W$, scalar readout $W + 1$, matching the implementation's own count ---
does not grow with $\nsites$. Its receptive radius is
$r$ by construction: no amount of data lets it propagate information further.

\paragraph{Dilated convolutional stack.} A residual stack of three-tap
convolutions at geometrically increasing dilation,
\begin{equation}
  h^{(\ell)}_k \;=\; h^{(\ell-1)}_k
  \;+\; P_\ell \tanh\!\Big(\textstyle\sum_{s \in \{-d_\ell,\,0,\,d_\ell\}} W_{\ell,s}\, h^{(\ell-1)}_{k+s} + b_\ell\Big),
  \qquad d_\ell \in \{1,2,4,8\},
  \label{eq:em-dilated}
\end{equation}
initialised from $h^{(0)} = \tanh(W_{\mathrm{in}}[x;\tau(t)])$ and read out by a
pointwise map. Its receptive radius is $\sum_\ell d_\ell = 15$, which spans an
$\nsites = 32$ chain end to end.

\paragraph{Bidirectional message passing.} Forward and backward recurrences with
shared local updates and a joint readout,
\begin{equation}
  \overrightarrow{h}_k = \tanh\!\big(A z_k + F \overrightarrow{h}_{k-1} + a\big),
  \quad
  \overleftarrow{h}_k = \tanh\!\big(B z_k + G \overleftarrow{h}_{k+1} + b\big),
  \quad
  \widehat{y}_k = C\big[\overrightarrow{h}_k; \overleftarrow{h}_k; z_k\big] + c,
  \label{eq:em-bimp}
\end{equation}
where $z_k = [x_k; \tau(t)]$. This is the same topology as the two-sweep
sum--product schedule of Section~\ref{sec:sum-product}: one local rule applied
at every site, run once in each direction, then combined --- with affine-plus-%
$\tanh$ updates in place of the exact message updates. Of the three it has the
best claim to being able to \emph{learn} what belief propagation computes, and
its recurrent blocks are initialised orthogonally so that a perturbation at one
end of the chain is not attenuated to nothing before it reaches the other.

\section{Pointwise data efficiency}\label{sec:em-pointwise}

Against the unstructured denoiser \eqref{eq:em-mlp}, and under the structural
assumptions of Table~\ref{tab:em-protocol}, the structured estimator attains
lower relative denoising error by a factor of $\ratiolo$--$\ratiohi$ across the
\nsizesused{} training-set sizes (Table~\ref{tab:pointwise},
Figure~\ref{fig:em-value-of-structure}a). It is more accurate in all but $\nreversed$ of
the $\ncellsused$ scored (seed, size, level) cells. The factor is not
monotone in the budget; Appendix~\ref{app:aggregation} reports the same
cells under four alternative summaries, of which the retired
mean-of-per-level-ratios is systematically the largest.

The growth of the ratio has a one-line reading that the panel supports directly.
If each arm's error follows a power law $\mathcal{E} \approx c\,\nseq^{-\varrho}$
over the tested range, then
\begin{equation}
  R(\nseq) \;=\; \frac{c_{\mathrm{base}}}{c_{\mathrm{EM}}}\,
  \nseq^{\,\varrho_{\mathrm{EM}} - \varrho_{\mathrm{base}}},
  \label{eq:em-rate-ratio}
\end{equation}
so the ratio grows precisely when the structured arm converges faster in data.
The fitted finite-range slopes of the pooled error curves are
$\varrho_{\mathrm{EM}} = \pooledslopeem \pm \pooledslopeemse$ and
$\varrho_{\mathrm{base}} = \pooledslopebase \pm \pooledslopebasese$
(log--log least squares over the
\nsizesused{} sizes --- descriptive fits, not asymptotic rates), and
\eqref{eq:em-rate-ratio} then predicts
that a $128$-fold increase in data multiplies the ratio by
$128^{\pooledslopeem - \pooledslopebase} = \pooledgrowthpred$; the measured ratio moves from
$\ratiofirst$ to $\ratiolast$, a factor of $\ratiogrowth$,
non-monotonically. Two remarks travel with that. Neither slope is the parametric
$\varrho = 1/2$, so neither arm is behaving like a correctly specified
finite-dimensional estimator over this range --- for EM--BP the mixture is
misspecified against a Laplace innovation and the grid is finite; for the
network the function class is unrestricted. And \eqref{eq:em-rate-ratio}
describes eight points; it identifies no mechanism.

Every tunable quantity either arm is allowed to choose is chosen on a validation
bundle disjoint from both training and test, and the two arms are given the same
privilege: the network selects its output head \emph{and} its training length,
EM--BP its iteration count. That choice coincides with the test-set optimum in
$\netagree\%$ of cells for the network and $\emagree\%$ for EM--BP. 

\section{How much of the margin is the architecture?}\label{sec:em-architecture}

\subsection{The bulk posterior, in closed form}\label{sec:em-locality-prediction}

Everything in this subsection is exact for the variance-matched Gaussian chain
and is used here as a \emph{design calculation} for the Laplace chain, whose
second-order structure it shares. How wide a
window does a score head need? We tried to answer with arithmetic before any
network is trained.

\paragraph{Step 1: the channel adds only to the diagonal.} The prior is
$a_k = \corr a_{k-1} + \eta_k$ with $\eta_k \sim \Nd(0,\ivar)$ and
$\ivar = 1-\corr^2$, so the marginal variance is one and
$\Sigz = (\corr^{|i-j|})$. Its precision $Q_0 = \Sigz^{-1}$ is tridiagonal ---
the Markov property written in matrix form ---
\begin{equation}
  [Q_0]_{kk} = \frac{1+\corr^2}{\ivar}\ \ \text{(interior)},
  \qquad
  [Q_0]_{k,k\pm1} = -\frac{\corr}{\ivar},
  \qquad
  [Q_0]_{kj} = 0 \ \ (|k-j| \geq 2).
  \label{eq:em-prior-precision}
\end{equation}
The channel contributes $-\tfrac{1}{2\Deltat}\norm{x - e^{-t}a}^2$ to the
log-posterior. Collecting the quadratic and linear terms in $a$,
\begin{equation}
  -\log p(a \mid x) \;=\; \tfrac12\, a^{\T} J a \;-\; h^{\T} a \;+\; \text{const},
  \qquad
  J \;=\; Q_0 + \frac{e^{-2t}}{\Deltat}\,I,
  \qquad
  h \;=\; \frac{e^{-t}}{\Deltat}\,x,
  \label{eq:em-posterior-precision}
\end{equation}
so the posterior is Gaussian with mean $m = \E[a\mid x] = J^{-1}h$. The
structural fact that drives the whole subsection is visible in
\eqref{eq:em-posterior-precision}: \emph{the channel enters the diagonal
alone.} It stiffens every site against its own observation and leaves the
nearest-neighbour couplings exactly as the prior set them. In the bulk --- away
from the two ends, where \eqref{eq:em-prior-precision} loses one neighbour ---
$J$ is therefore tridiagonal Toeplitz,
\begin{equation}
  J \;=\; \operatorname{tridiag}(\beta,\, J_{\mathrm{d}},\, \beta),
  \qquad
  J_{\mathrm{d}} \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{e^{-2t}}{\Deltat}}_{\text{evidence}}
  \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{1+\corr^2}{\ivar}}_{\text{prior}},
  \qquad
  \beta \;=\; -\frac{\corr}{\ivar}.
  \label{eq:em-bulk-params}
\end{equation}
Only $J_{\mathrm{d}}$ depends on $t$, and it is monotone: it diverges like
$1/(2t)$ as $t \downarrow 0$ and falls to the prior value as $t \to \infty$.

\paragraph{Step 2: inverting a tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix.} A matrix with three
bands has an inverse that is dense, but on the bulk of a long chain that dense
inverse is a single geometric sequence.

\begin{proposition}[Bulk covariance of the chain posterior]
\label{prop:em-bulk}
In the interior of a long chain the inverse of \eqref{eq:em-bulk-params} is
\begin{equation}
  \big(J^{-1}\big)_{k,\,k+d} \;=\; V\,q^{|d|},
  \qquad
  q \;=\; \frac{J_{\mathrm{d}} - \sqrt{J_{\mathrm{d}}^2 - 4\beta^2}}{2|\beta|} \in (0,1),
  \qquad
  V \;=\; \frac{1}{\sqrt{J_{\mathrm{d}}^2 - 4\beta^2}},
  \label{eq:em-bulk-cov}
\end{equation}
and the square root is real for every $|\corr| < 1$ and every $t > 0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{toolbox}{Transfer-matrix inversion of a tridiagonal Toeplitz matrix}
Write $G = J^{-1}$ and look for a solution of the defining relation
$\sum_j J_{ij}G_{j,k} = \delta_{ik}$ in geometric form,
$G_{i,i+d} = V q^{|d|}$, symmetric in $\pm d$. With $i$ interior, the sum has
only three terms, and setting $k = i+d$ with $d \geq 1$ gives
\begin{equation*}
  \beta\, V q^{\,d+1} \;+\; J_{\mathrm{d}}\, V q^{\,d} \;+\; \beta\, V q^{\,d-1} \;=\; 0
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \beta q^2 + J_{\mathrm{d}}\, q + \beta \;=\; 0 ,
\end{equation*}
a quadratic that is the same for every $d$ --- which is why one geometric
sequence suffices. Its two roots multiply to $\beta/\beta = 1$, so they are
$q$ and $1/q$: \emph{exactly one} lies inside the unit disc, and it is the one
above. (For $\corr > 0$ all entries are positive; for $\corr < 0$ they alternate
in sign with the same modulus.) The remaining relation, at $d = 0$, fixes the
prefactor: $J_{\mathrm{d}} V + 2\beta V q = 1$, and since
$2\beta q = -\big(J_{\mathrm{d}} - \sqrt{J_{\mathrm{d}}^2 - 4\beta^2}\big)$ this
reads $V\sqrt{J_{\mathrm{d}}^2 - 4\beta^2} = 1$.

Reality of the root needs $J_{\mathrm{d}} > 2|\beta|$, and the margin is
strictly positive at every operating point,
\begin{equation*}
  J_{\mathrm{d}} - 2|\beta|
  \;=\; \frac{e^{-2t}}{\Deltat} + \frac{1 + \corr^2 - 2|\corr|}{\ivar}
  \;=\; \frac{e^{-2t}}{\Deltat} + \frac{(1 - |\corr|)^2}{\ivar}
  \;>\; 0 ,
\end{equation*}
both terms being non-negative and the first strictly positive for $t > 0$. So
$q$ is real, and $q < 1$ strictly. \hfill$\square$
\end{toolbox}

\noindent Two checks. The formula is verified against a direct numerical
inverse at $\nsites = 32$, $\corr = 0.85$: the relative disagreement over
$d = 0,\dots,5$ is $6\times10^{-15}$ at $t = 0.05$ and $2.7\times10^{-4}$ at
$t = 3$, the growth being the two boundaries leaking into the ``bulk'' once the
correlation length is an appreciable fraction of the chain --- which is the one
regime in which \eqref{eq:em-bulk-cov} should be read as approximate here. And
the limits are the ones physical sense demands,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
  t \to 0:&\quad J_{\mathrm{d}} \to \infty,
  &&q \approx \frac{|\beta|}{J_{\mathrm{d}}} \to 0,
  &&V \to 0
  &&\text{(sites pinned by their observations)};\\
  t \to \infty:&\quad J_{\mathrm{d}} \to \frac{1+\corr^2}{\ivar},
  &&q \to |\corr|,
  &&V \to 1
  &&\text{(posterior}\to\text{prior, unit variance)},
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:em-q-limits}
\end{equation}
the second because $J_{\mathrm{d}}^2 - 4\beta^2 = (1-\corr^2)^2/\sigma_\eta^4 = 1$
once the evidence term vanishes. As diffusion time runs, the posterior
correlation length climbs monotonically from zero to the prior's own.

\paragraph{Step 3: the influence of distant evidence is exactly geometric.}
Because $m = \frac{e^{-t}}{\Deltat}J^{-1}x$ is linear in the observation,
Proposition~\ref{prop:em-bulk} gives the sensitivity of one site's posterior
mean to an observation $d$ sites away in closed form,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\partial m_k}{\partial x_{k \pm d}}
  \;=\; \frac{e^{-t}}{\Deltat}\,\big(J^{-1}\big)_{k,\,k\pm d}
  \;=\; \frac{e^{-t}}{\Deltat}\, V\, q^{\,d}
  \;=\; \frac{e^{-t} V}{\Deltat}\, e^{-d/\xi},
  \qquad
  \xi \;\coloneqq\; \frac{1}{\log(1/q)} .
  \label{eq:em-influence}
\end{equation}
This is the object a score head has to represent, and it is a pure exponential
in the separation with a single length $\xi(\corr,t)$. Nothing about it is
fitted, and within the infinite-chain bulk nothing about it is asymptotic in
$t$ either; on a finite chain it is the bulk approximation, exact in the
interior and corrected near the two ends, as Chapter~\ref{ch:gaussian-bp}
derives it.

\paragraph{Step 4: what a radius-$r$ window must discard.} A head of radius $r$
cannot use $x_{k\pm d}$ for $d > r$, whatever it learns. In squared weight,
using \eqref{eq:em-influence} and two geometric sums,
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\sum_{|d| > r} q^{2|d|}}{\sum_{d \in \mathbb{Z}} q^{2|d|}}
  \;=\;
  \frac{2q^{2(r+1)}/(1-q^2)}{(1+q^2)/(1-q^2)}
  \;=\;
  \frac{2\,q^{2(r+1)}}{1+q^2} ,
  \label{eq:em-discarded}
\end{equation}
exactly. Pooling over the schedule with the closed-form weights of
Table~\ref{tab:em-locality} gives one number per radius, the
\emph{coefficient-tail fraction}
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{T}(r) \;=\; \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} w_t \;\frac{2\,q(\corr,t)^{2(r+1)}}{1 + q(\corr,t)^2} .
  \label{eq:em-truncation}
\end{equation}
What \eqref{eq:em-truncation} is, and is not, has to be exact.
It is a fraction of squared coefficient mass, and it sets the geometric
\emph{exponent} of everything local. It is \emph{not} a prediction risk, for
three reasons: the omitted observations are correlated with the retained
ones, so their squared coefficients do not add to an error energy; a genuine
windowed estimator reoptimises its retained coefficients --- it is the
posterior mean of the window model, not the exact coefficients with the tail
set to zero; and a squared-mass fraction is not commensurable with a relative
RMS error even in scale. It is used below only as a decay diagnostic.

The valid risk anchor exists in closed form and costs nothing. The
Bayes-optimal radius-$r$ estimator of the design model is the windowed
posterior mean $\E[a_k \mid x_{k-r},\dots,x_{k+r}]$ --- exactly the
windowed inference whose price Chapter~\ref{ch:gaussian-bp} computes --- and
its population relative score error, pooled over the schedule with the same
weights, is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{W}(r)
  \;=\;
  \Bigg(
  \frac{\sum_{t} \operatorname{tr}\big[(M_r(t)-\Qt)\,\Sigt\,(M_r(t)-\Qt)^{\T}\big]}
       {\sum_{t} \operatorname{tr}\Qt}
  \Bigg)^{1/2},
  \label{eq:em-oracle}
\end{equation}
$-M_r(t)x$ being the windowed score estimator. This is the exact
local-oracle error of the Gaussian design calculation: no learning, no
sampling, the best any radius-$r$ estimator can do \emph{on the
covariance-matched Gaussian model}. Numerically, at this $(\corr,\nsites)$
and schedule,

\emph{Prediction.} Under this protocol the best window radius is small ---
of order the largest correlation length that carries appreciable weight, a
few sites --- and the risk should \emph{rise} again well beyond it, because
past that point the receptive field is buying a quantity smaller than the
estimation error it costs. And the residual error of the selected head should
sit well above the Gaussian design oracle $\mathcal{W}(r)$ at its radius,
because at small radii the window itself is no longer what limits a learned
head.
\end{quote}

\section{Where the Markov assumption stops paying}\label{sec:em-nonmarkov}

Every result so far assumes the clean chain is exactly first-order Markov. Two
controlled violations are measured against an exact reference, so that the
estimator being wrong is not confused with the reference being wrong.

\subsection{Two violations, defined}

\paragraph{A shared global latent, strength $\beta$.} One standard normal
variable is drawn per sequence and added to every site,
\begin{equation}
  a \;=\; \frac{y + \beta g}{\sqrt{1+\beta^2}},
  \qquad y \sim \text{chain prior},
  \quad g \sim \Nd(0,1),
  \label{eq:em-global}
\end{equation}
variance preserving for every $\beta$ and Markov only at $\beta = 0$. Its
covariance is
$\Sig_\beta = (\Sigz + \beta^2 \mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\T)/(1+\beta^2)$ with
$\Sigz$ the AR(1) covariance, so a Laplace and a Gaussian version differ
\emph{only beyond second moments} --- the controlled comparison this thesis uses
throughout. At $\beta = 1$ half the marginal variance of every site is one
shared constant.

\paragraph{Long-range precision coupling, strength $\gamma$.} Weak couplings are
added directly to the precision at every separation of two or more,
\begin{equation}
  Q_\gamma \;=\; Q_{\mathrm{AR}} + \gamma\,Q_{\mathrm{long}},
  \qquad
  [Q_{\mathrm{long}}]_{ij} = -e^{-|i-j|/\ell}\ \ (|i-j| \geq 2),
  \quad
  [Q_{\mathrm{long}}]_{ii} = 1.05 \sum_{|i-j| \geq 2} e^{-|i-j|/\ell},
  \label{eq:em-longrange}
\end{equation}
with $\ell = 8$ and the diagonal compensation making $Q_\gamma$ strictly
diagonally dominant, hence positive definite; the covariance is rescaled to unit
diagonal so the prior stays variance preserving. Not Markov for
$\gamma \neq 0$.

\subsection{Why one is partly absorbable and the other is not}

The two mechanisms are not two amounts of the same thing.

\begin{proposition}[The global latent perturbs the chain in one direction]
\label{prop:em-rankone}
Let $Q_{\mathrm{AR}} = \Sigz^{-1}$ be the AR(1) precision and
$Q_\beta = \Sig_\beta^{-1}$. Then
\begin{equation}
  Q_\beta \;=\; (1+\beta^2)\,Q_{\mathrm{AR}} \;-\; c_\beta\, u u^{\T},
  \qquad
  u = Q_{\mathrm{AR}}\mathbf{1},
  \qquad
  c_\beta = \frac{(1+\beta^2)\,\beta^2}{1 + \beta^2\,\mathbf{1}^{\T}Q_{\mathrm{AR}}\mathbf{1}},
  \label{eq:em-woodbury}
\end{equation}
so the departure from a rescaled chain precision has rank exactly one, in the
single direction $u$. Moreover the lag-one correlation of \eqref{eq:em-global},
and hence the correlation of its best first-order chain approximation, is
available in closed form:
\begin{equation}
  \corr_{\mathrm{CL}}(\beta) \;=\; \frac{\corr + \beta^2}{1+\beta^2}.
  \label{eq:em-chowliu}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Sherman--Morrison applied to
$\Sig_\beta = (\Sigz + \beta^2\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\T)/(1+\beta^2)$ gives
\eqref{eq:em-woodbury} directly, the rank-one term being the correction that
inverting a rank-one update produces. For \eqref{eq:em-chowliu}, the covariance
of \eqref{eq:em-global} at separation $d \geq 1$ is
$(\corr^{d} + \beta^2)/(1+\beta^2)$ and the marginal variance is $1$; at
$d = 1$ this is the stated value. The KL projection of a Gaussian onto a fixed
tree matches that tree's node and edge marginals
\citep{chow1968approximating}, so on a path graph the fitted chain reproduces
the true adjacent correlations. \hfill$\square$
\end{proof}

\noindent Numerically, \eqref{eq:em-woodbury} is exact to machine precision: the
correction has one nonzero eigenvalue at every $\beta$ tested, and its
eigenvector agrees with $u/\norm{u}$ to $|\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle| = 1.000000$.
The direction $u$ is, up to boundary terms, the constant vector --- the chain's
mean level. By contrast the correction in \eqref{eq:em-longrange} is
$\gamma Q_{\mathrm{long}}$, dense by construction, with numerical rank
$\nsites = 32$ and all $32$ eigenvalues above $1\%$ of its largest at the
strengths tested. \emph{One direction against thirty} is the structural
difference, and a chain that may reparameterise a single scalar can trade
against the first in a way it cannot against the second --- partly: matching
the lag-one correlation absorbs the covariance change along that one
direction, but a homogeneous first-order chain cannot represent the dense
rank-one precision correction exactly, so ``absorbable'' means \emph{partly
absorbed through the refitted local parameter}, not reproduced.

The fitted coefficient does exactly that. Under \eqref{eq:em-global} the fitted
$\widehat{\corr}$ climbs from $\nmrhoclean$ on the clean chain to
$\nmrhobetamax$ at $\beta = \nmbetamax$, tracking \eqref{eq:em-chowliu}
($0.9250$ at $\beta = 1$) to within $\nmcldevbeta$. Under
\eqref{eq:em-longrange} the same gap is $\nmcldevfactor$ times larger. That is
one scalar agreeing, not the whole fitted law certified --- but it is the
mechanism the proposition predicts, appearing in the fit.

\subsection{A covariance scalar orders the tested conditions}

Proposition~\ref{prop:em-rankone} explains the qualitative difference. A single
number makes it quantitative. The scalar used is the per-site KL divergence
between the \emph{covariance-matched centred Gaussians} of the contaminated
law and of its best first-order chain,
\begin{equation}
  \delta \;=\; \frac{1}{\nsites}\,
  \kl{\Nd(0,\Sig)}{\Nd(0,\Sig_{\mathrm{CL}})}
  \;=\; \frac{1}{2\nsites}\Big[
  \operatorname{tr}\big(\Sig_{\mathrm{CL}}^{-1}\Sig\big) - \nsites
  + \log\frac{\det \Sig_{\mathrm{CL}}}{\det \Sig}\Big],
  \label{eq:em-delta}
\end{equation}
closed-form for both mechanisms and involving no fit and no data. Its status
differs by arm, and the distinction is not cosmetic. For the \emph{Gaussian}
contaminated laws, \eqref{eq:em-delta} is the exact distributional KL to the
best Gaussian chain --- the excess code length that the Gaussian Chow--Liu
projection minimises. For the \emph{Laplace} arms the same expression is only
a covariance proxy: the true laws are non-Gaussian, their KL involves entropy
terms that covariance does not determine, and two non-Gaussian laws with
identical covariances can sit at different true divergences. Where the
distinction matters we write $\delta_{\mathrm{cov}}$ for the proxy reading;
it is a second-order misspecification measure, blind to everything beyond
covariance, and that is exactly the information the chain estimator's Gaussian
design geometry uses.

\captionof{table}[Misspecification, measured in nats, orders both

coupling the advantage against the convolutional baseline is gone by
$\gamma = \nmcrossover$, having survived $\gamma = \nmlastholding$, and by
$\gamma = 0.2$ the estimator loses to both baselines and not to one; at
$\gamma = \nmgammamax$ its own relative score error reaches $\nmemworsterr\%$.

Read against $\delta$, the tested conditions collapse onto one empirical
curve. The nine contaminated
cells interleave --- $\beta = 1$ sits between $\gamma = 0$ and
$\gamma = 0.05$ --- and the advantage is monotone in $\delta$ across both
mechanisms, over
four orders of magnitude in $\delta$ and a factor of twenty in the ratio,
against both baselines and for both innovation laws. The crossing is reported
as the bracket the design actually resolves, between adjacent tested
strengths:
\begin{equation}
  \delta^{\star} \in \big[1.1, 2.2\big]\times 10^{-2}\ \text{nats/site (vs CNN)},
  \qquad
  \big[2.2, 4.0\big]\times 10^{-2}\ \text{nats/site (vs MLP)} ,
  \label{eq:em-breakeven}
\end{equation}
the CNN ratio falling from $1.2$ to $0.97$ across its bracket and the MLP one
from $1.2$ to $0.84$ across its own. A straight line through those endpoints
crosses at $2.1$ and $3.2 \times 10^{-2}$ respectively, and those
interpolations are quoted nowhere as results: each condition here is a single
run scored across the schedule, with no seed replication, so no interval can
be attached to a crossing and a point estimate would imply a precision the
design does not have. Three further scope conditions travel with the bracket.
The two mechanisms occupy different parts of the $\delta$ axis, so the
crossing is bracketed by different conditions in each case; the ratios in this
section are schedule means of per-level ratios rather than the pooled estimand
of \eqref{eq:em-pooling}, which changes their numerical scale though not their
ordering; and adjacent strengths differ by roughly a factor of two, which is
the resolution of the whole statement.
This collapse is an empirical regularity of the two violation paths
constructed here, not a sufficiency theorem, and there is a structural reason
to expect it to have limits: even between Gaussians, equal KL does not imply
equal score geometry --- the divergence is a spectral aggregate, while the
score error of a chain approximation is
$\operatorname{tr}[(Q_{\mathrm{chain}}-Q_{\mathrm{true}})\,\Sig\,
(Q_{\mathrm{chain}}-Q_{\mathrm{true}})^{\T}]$, which depends on \emph{where}
the precision error points. Two perturbations of matched $\delta$ whose
discrepancies live in different spatial modes could in principle separate, and
nothing here tests that. Equation \eqref{eq:em-breakeven} is interpolation
within the tested conditions, not a universal tolerance for non-Markov
structure.

\section{Why the shape coordinate is the expensive one}
\label{sec:em-shape-information}

Chapter~\ref{ch:em-kernel} found the innovation shape converging far slower than
the autoregressive coefficient, and attributed it to the fourth cumulant being
damped by the channel while the second is preserved. That is a claim about the
noised likelihood, and it can be checked directly against Fisher information,
independently of any optimiser.

\subsection{What the cumulant law predicts}

Cumulants add under independent sums and a Gaussian contributes only to the
second, so for $X_t = e^{-t}A + \sqrt{\Deltat}\,\varepsilon$,
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_2(X_t) = e^{-2t}\kappa_2(A) + \Deltat = 1,
  \qquad
  \kappa_4(X_t) = e^{-4t}\kappa_4(A),
  \qquad
  \kappa_{11}(X_{t,i}, X_{t,i+1}) = e^{-2t}\corr,
  \label{eq:em-cumulants}
\end{equation}
 Now consider what identifies each
coordinate. The autoregressive coefficient is second order: it enters through
the lag-one cumulant, which the channel attenuates by $e^{-2t}$. A shape
parameter --- one that moves the fourth cumulant at exactly fixed variance --- is
fourth order, and the channel attenuates its signature by $e^{-4t}$. Reading
information as squared sensitivity over sampling variance --- a delta-method
heuristic, not a likelihood calculation: it treats the cumulant estimator as
the inference channel and its variance as approximately constant in $t$ ---
\begin{equation}
  I_\theta \;\asymp\;
  \frac{\big(\partial_\theta \kappa(X_t)\big)^2}{\operatorname{Var}\widehat{\kappa}},
\end{equation}
and noting that the denominators are $t$-independent \emph{to leading order}
because
$\kappa_2(X_t) \equiv 1$ pins the scale of the noised variable at every $t$
(higher moments of the noised law do drift with $t$, which is part of what the
heuristic omits),
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  I_{\corr}(t) \;\asymp\; e^{-4t},
  \qquad
  I_{p}(t) \;\asymp\; e^{-8t},
  \qquad
  \frac{I_{p}(t)}{I_{\corr}(t)} \;\asymp\; e^{-4t}.
  \;}
  \label{eq:em-info-prediction}
\end{equation}
The third statement is the robust one: it is a \emph{contrast}, so whatever
common factors the leading-order reading omits cancel from it.

\subsection{The measurement}

The prediction is tested on a family in which shape moves at exactly fixed
variance. The innovation law is a generalised Gaussian,
$f_p(\eta) \propto \exp(-|\eta/s|^{p})$, with $s$ fixed by
$\operatorname{Var}(\eta) = \ivar = 1-\corr^2$ at every $p$, so $p$ is a genuine
shape coordinate and not a reparameterised scale; $p = 1$ is the Laplace chain
of Chapter~\ref{ch:laplace}, $p = 2$ Gaussian. For
$\theta = (\corr, \ivar, p)$, Fisher's identity \eqref{eq:em-fisher} supplies
the exact per-sequence score of the marginal log-likelihood from one
forward--backward pass, and the information matrix is estimated in
outer-product form $J = D^{-1}\sum_d g_d g_d^{\T}$ over $D = 512$ sequences of
$\nsites = 32$ sites at $\ngrid = 401$, $A = 8$. The quantity reported is the
\emph{efficient} information $I_{p\cdot(\corr,\ivar)} = 1/(J^{-1})_{pp}$, what
remains about $p$ once the nuisance coordinates must be estimated too --- equal
to the diagonal entry only when the coordinates are orthogonal and strictly
smaller otherwise.

Two things follow from that construction and travel with every number below.
An outer-product matrix is the Fisher information only at the true parameter
of a correctly specified model, and it is here a $D$-sample estimate of it, so
these are estimates with sampling error and not an exact information
calculation. And the reported quantity is an \emph{inverse}, so its meaning
degrades with the conditioning of $J$ --- which the run records per cell.
That conditioning is benign exactly where the information is large and fails
exactly where it vanishes: $\kappa(J) = \infcondbest$ at
$t = \infcondbestt$ on the Laplace chain, a median of $\infcondmedian$ across
the sweep, and $\infcondworst$ in the worst cell
($t = \infcondworstt$, $p = \infcondworstp$), where the efficient information
is itself down at $\infcondworstinfo$. The decay these curves measure is
therefore resolved at the end where the coordinate is identified, and the far
tail is a statement that the information has gone, not a measurement of how
much remains.

\noindent Table~\ref{tab:em-verdicts} states the four findings at the strength
their evidence supports. Three consequences carry into
Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusions}. Supplying the
generating family is worth a large factor at every data budget tested, but
most of the headline factor does not survive a baseline that is merely given
locality and weight sharing --- which bounds how much of it can be attributed
specifically to the Markov model, without decomposing causally what the swap
of baselines changes. Across the tested violations, the measured advantage
ordered monotonically with a covariance scalar computable before any fitting
--- an empirical regularity of the two paths probed, useful as a screening
variable and not established as a decision rule. And the coordinate on which
every non-Gaussian claim in this
thesis depends is simultaneously the slowest to converge and the poorest in
information per observation, which is why the budget disclosures in this chapter
are part of its results rather than an appendix to them.
